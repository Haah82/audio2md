Underground erosion through earth dams is a frequent occurrence in medium and small dams in Vietnam, especially at the interface between the concrete structure and the embankment soil.

This is a vulnerable point where seepage and underground erosion are very likely to occur.

This is a common and difficult problem to address due to the nature of soil compaction during construction.

In particular, the water intake culvert is a frequent site of incidents causing landslides, subsidence, or even collapse.

The main reason is that the water intake culvert is usually located deep beneath the dam, where it is subjected to high soil pressure and water head.

Furthermore, the shape of the culvert structure affects the soil compaction method, so the area around the culvert is often not compacted, making the soil more susceptible to seepage and erosion.

Some specific causes of contact erosion include: underground culverts located deep beneath the dam, often failing to adequately drain the foundation pit before concrete pouring; in many cases, concrete is poured when the foundation pit has not been properly drained, reducing the quality of the concrete and the quality of the contact layer between the foundation and the structure; the side walls of the culvert are difficult to compact because the roller cannot access them.

Conventional design guidelines often instruct the use of manual compaction, but manual compaction is unlikely to achieve the same level of compaction as machine compaction.

Therefore, the area around the culvert is prone to seepage and erosion, which over time will form large erosion pipes, causing serious consequences for circular culverts.

The area at the base of the culvert is the most difficult to work on because it is impossible to bring in machinery for compaction, and manual compaction is also very difficult to ensure construction according to the design.

In addition, bringing in machinery to compact the soil next to the culvert can cause the culvert to rise, leading to joint leaks and culvert breakage, further exacerbating seepage along the culvert body.

With a flat culvert ceiling, the embankment's weight will transfer to the ceiling through the arch effect, meaning that a zone of soil under the arch will form without additional pressure, resulting in lower compaction.

This area also often does not achieve the design compaction and is therefore prone to seepage.

To limit the impact of uneven settlement, the culvert is usually divided into many segments, each from 30 to 50 m.

However, the joints are the most vulnerable points to damage, causing water leakage outside the dam, especially for culverts with water pressure.

Seepage in the culvert easily occurs, increasing the phenomenon of contact erosion along the culvert body.

For non-pressurized culverts, the seepage flow from the outside carries soil particles into the culvert, hollowing out the dam body and causing land subsidence.

To limit contact seepage along the culvert, baffles are designed to extend the seepage path.

However, the design of these baffles also faces many problems due to actual seepage.

In reality, dam failures due to contact seepage along the culvert have been observed.

It has been concluded that rigid baffles should only be placed in the upstream half of the culvert, while the downstream half should only have flexible baffles that filter seepage water.

Contact seepage due to design and construction issues can cause dam failure immediately upon water impoundment or in the first year after the reservoir is full.

For culverts that have been in operation for many years, seepage usually occurs due to defects in the culvert itself, such as culvert holes due to defects or concrete degradation, or leaks in the culvert joints.

In addition, seepage along the spillway wall has similar causes, usually due to the filter holes on the spillway wall becoming ineffective, causing voids in the soil along the wall.

When detecting leaks around the culvert, the first thing to check is whether mud and soil are being carried out.

If the seepage water is cloudy and contains soil particles, it is possible that the dam body has suffered internal erosion.

This is a serious situation requiring urgent action.

The first thing the manager needs to do is create a filter layer at the inlet of the seepage flow.

For long-term repair, a detailed design is needed, usually involving drilling and grouting for waterproofing combined with reinforcing the contact layer with special materials.
