Japan is not as rich as it looks. Many people here work every day and still struggling to afford a normal life. 

I work every day too, but to save money, I sometimes wait at the supermarket for about 30 minutes until they put discount stickers on the food. I walk around the store with an empty basket. I pretend to look at the other things, then go back to the same shelf. Other people are sometimes waiting, too. 

When the staff starts putting on the stickers, the discounted food disappears very quickly. Sometimes I wait for 30 minutes and still cannot get what I wanted. All of this just to save a few hundred yen. 

It may sound strange, but when money is tight, even 200 or 300 yen matters. And I don't think I'm the only young person living like this. In Japan, many young people take home only around 150,000 yen a month after taxes, health insurance, and pension payments are taken out. 

But when you visit Japan, it may not look like a poor country at all. The streets are clean, the food is good, the trains are convenient, and Japan is a very safe country. 

People wear clean clothes and go to work as usual, so just by looking at someone, you cannot tell that they are struggling or poor. But after paying rent, utilities, food, transportation, there may be almost nothing left. 

You may work full-time and still think carefully before buying new clothes. You may avoid eating out. You may stay home on the days off, because going outside costs money. You may have a home, you may look completely normal, but you still worry about money every day. This is the kind of poverty that is difficult to see. 

And this is not only about low salaries. About one in three workers has a non-regular job, such as part-time, temporary, or contract work with less job security. And about one in two university students has debt in the form of a student loan. 

In Japan, these student loans are called scholarships, but many of them must be paid back after graduation. So many young people begin their working lives already in debt. So why do young people in Japan take home so little money?

Taxes, health insurance, and pension payments are taken from our salaries. Of course, these systems are necessary, but when your salary is already low, every payment feels heavy. 

And even if you quit your job, the bills do not stop. Even when you have no income, you still receive bills for health insurance, pension payments, and resident tax based on your income from the year before. You have no income, but the payments continue. 

Many young people are also worried about the pension system. We have to keep paying into it, but we do not know whether we will receive enough to live on when we are older. 

At the same time, Japan has become very cheap for many foreign visitors. Depending on where you come from, food and shopping in Japan may feel much cheaper than in your own country. For some people, it may even feel close to half the price. 

I understand why Japan is such a popular tourist destination, but visiting Japan and living here are completely different. If you earn dollars or euros, Japan may feel cheap. But for people earning Japanese yen, Japan does not feel like cheap at all. 

Prices keep rising, but salaries are not rising at the same speed. So people slowly give things up: eating out, traveling, getting married, having children. 

From the outside, Japan may still look like a wealthy country, but many people here are simply trying to make it through each month. This is poverty in Japan that you cannot see just by looking at someone. That is what life feels like for many people in Japan right now.
