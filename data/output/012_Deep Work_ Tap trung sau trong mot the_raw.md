So, how to block out tasks, schedule internet usage, establish a ritual for ending the workday, measure the number of deep work hours per week, and have valid reasons to say no to shallow work and social media are tips I 've used myself and found very useful, practical, easy to apply, not theoretical, and especially helpful in today's society with so many distractions.

However, why am I only giving this book 3 out of 5 stars?

Firstly, the tips I mentioned above are actually just a few of the really boring parts of the book that I've omitted.

However, it is inevitable that there are areas where it needs improvement.

But most notably, I think the first part is that it's overly idealized and doesn't apply to all professions.

I have a feeling this book was written for academics or middle to high-level office workers.

This is especially true for those who have autonomy over their time, something most of us working people don't have much control over.

This is especially true for those working in sales, call centers, receptionists, nurses, doctors, and teachers.

How can we possibly not reply to messages, or not respond to messages, not interact with people around us, not interact with students , not interact with patients, not answer the phone for 3 to 4 hours, not reply to emails?

Therefore, I believe that professions that require working in complex environments or involving complex interpersonal communication do not have the option of isolating themselves to engage in deep work.

Secondly, I found the book to have a lot of repetitive ideas .

I've lost focus while reading a few times.

There are some sections that I find really good, but many others I feel could be combined, or many sections are like two or more ideas written in different ways.

Among the four main principles in this book, I find that the second principle is to endure boredom, to embrace boredom, and the third principle is to quit social media.

I think these two principles can be combined or significantly shortened.

Another drawback is that I find scheduling down to the minute a bit extreme.

Sometimes, when I get caught up in the flow of work, forgetting who's around me, what's happening, whether it's raining or sunny, or what time it is, I just ignore the idea of time blocking.

For example, while I was writing the script for this video, I remember I only spent about 1 hour to 1 hour and 15 minutes on it.

But when I finished writing it, I realized I was going to spend two hours on it and I couldn't possibly have a problem with that .

Because during those two hours, I was still dedicating 100% of my time to doing the maximum I could, right?

And I think many people would agree with me on this point of view.

For example, gamers like us can't just say we sit down to play games for 30 minutes; we could easily play for an hour or two without even realizing it.

Or, for creative people , artists, singers, painters, we can't tell them, "You're singing badly, you're painting badly, stop and go do something else," because we've used time blocks for them, right?

Therefore, I find this book a bit too academic and rigid in that respect.

Furthermore, this book completely ignores what we should be doing about mental aspects like stress, burnout, or ADHD.

We believe this book is somewhat rigid and relies heavily on formulas.

And finally, there's shallow work.

In my opinion, shallow work isn't as bad as the author says.

I think answering emails, attending meetings, or writing reports are also ways to push our menstrual cycle further.

We connect information, connect people, and elevate our solutions from the ordinary to the extraordinary.

Of course, the spirit of this book is very commendable, but living like a monk sometimes means being in seclusion, giving up social media, and meticulously scheduling every minute.

In some ways, I find it a bit extreme because the interaction and connection between people through social media is also a way for us to live more fulfilling lives, isn't it ?

In short, I still think this is a good book, but we should read it with an open mind.

Not all self-help books are empty platitudes, but just because someone says something brilliant—a professor teaching programming at a top university—doesn't mean it's necessarily true, right?

I've gathered many valuable lessons that I've applied and found to be very effective.

As for anything that I find illogical, I simply don't use it.

And I hope you find this book just as interesting.
